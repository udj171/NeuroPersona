What you actually get back

Six things this assessment does that a personality quiz usually does not. Each one follows from the same starting assumption: that the person answering has a stake in the answer.

Feature one

It adjusts for the answer you wanted to give

Five of the fifty-five statements are never scored as traits. Their job is to catch the moments where your answers describe someone slightly better than anyone actually is.

Each of those five is compared against the five trait scores. A high score paired with a cross-check that should have moved with it, and didn't, is a contradiction. Contradictions are counted, and the count feeds into how much correction is applied.

Feature two

You are shown both sets of numbers, not the tidier one

The correction is an argument, not a fact, so hiding the originals would be dishonest. Your results keep the scores as you gave them, the scores after adjustment, the size of the adjustment, and which traits it moved.

That means you can disagree with the method and still use the output. If you think the correction has read you wrong, the uncorrected profile is right there to read instead.

Feature three

A profile, not just a label

The pattern name on your results is a summary of five numbers, and a summary always throws information away. The five numbers underneath are the part worth reading.

Two people can share a pattern and differ sharply in the traits that produced it. That is why it comes with a confidence figure rather than as a category you belong to, and why the trait scores get more room on the page than the name does.

Feature four

Computed in one pass, then kept

Everything is worked out the moment you submit: the averages, the cross-check comparison, the adjustment, the pattern match, and the write-up. There is no queue and nothing to wait for afterwards.

All of it is stored against your assessment, so the link to your results reopens the same page with the same numbers rather than recomputing and drifting. The arithmetic steps are deterministic, which means the same answers always produce the same scores.

Feature five

It asks for almost nothing about you

There is no account, no sign-up, and no password. The assessment needs your age and your sex because both feed the scoring, and after that it only needs your answers.

Your record cannot be looked up by name or email, because neither is ever collected. What identifies an assessment is the link you are given at the end.

Being straight about what this is not. Collecting little is not the same as being secure, and this is a prototype rather than a hardened product. Treat your results link as semi-public: anyone who has it can open your results. Do not put anything in this assessment you would mind a stranger reading.

Feature six

The categories come from an argument, not a hunch

The five traits are not this project's invention, and that is the point: they are the Big Five, measured with a public-domain instrument that has been in use for decades. What is argued for here is the correction sitting on top of them, and that argument has a traceable lineage.

It starts with fieldwork on birds, becomes a general principle about signalling, gets applied to human self-knowledge in a book published by Oxford University Press, and ends in five awkward questions and a million-response dataset used to train the model that places your profile.

Where this differs from an ordinary quiz

None of the differences below are about being cleverer. They follow from one decision: treating your answers as evidence about a person with an interest in the outcome.

A typical personality quiz This assessment
What it does with your answers Takes them at face value and averages them. Estimates how much they were shaded, then adjusts each area by that estimate.
What the questions ask Whether you have a trait, and stops there. The same trait questions, plus five that ask how you are answering them.
What you are shown A label, and a paragraph about the label. Both score sets, the size of the correction, your nearest pattern, and your percentile in the reference sample.
Where the categories come from Convention, or a bespoke set with no reference data behind it. The Big Five, on a public instrument, with a million-response reference sample behind the model.
What it says about itself That it is accurate. That it is a prototype, and exactly where it stops being trustworthy.

The honest caveat, kept in view. This is a research prototype, not a validated psychological test and not a clinical instrument. It should not inform decisions about hiring, admissions, care, or anyone's standing. The full limits are here.
