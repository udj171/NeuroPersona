What you actually get back

Six things this assessment does that a personality quiz usually does not. Each one is a consequence of the same starting assumption: that the person answering has a stake in the answer.

Feature one

It adjusts for the answer you wanted to give

Five of the thirty-five statements are never scored as traits. Their job is to catch the moments where your answers describe someone slightly better than anyone actually is.

Each of those five is compared against the six trait scores. A high score paired with a cross-check that should have moved with it, and didn't, is a contradiction. Contradictions are counted, and the count sets how much correction is applied.

Feature two

You are shown both sets of numbers, not the tidier one

The correction is an argument, not a fact, so hiding the originals would be dishonest. Your results keep the scores as you gave them, the scores after adjustment, and the size of the adjustment itself.

That means you can disagree with the method and still use the output. If you think the correction has read you wrong, the uncorrected profile is right there to read instead.

Feature three

A profile, not just a label

The letter on your results is a summary of six numbers, and a summary always throws information away. The six numbers underneath are the part worth reading.

Two people can share a letter and differ sharply in the areas that produced it. That is why the type is presented with a confidence figure rather than as a category you belong to, and why the area scores get more room on the page than the letter does.

Feature four

Computed in one pass, then kept

Everything is worked out the moment you submit: the averages, the cross-check comparison, the adjustment, the pattern match, and the write-up. There is no queue and nothing to wait for afterwards.

All of it is stored against your assessment, so the link to your results reopens the same page with the same numbers rather than recomputing and drifting. The arithmetic steps are deterministic, which means the same answers always produce the same scores.

Feature five

It asks for almost nothing about you

There is no account, no sign-up, and no password. The assessment needs your age and your sex because both feed the scoring, and after that it only needs your answers.

Your record cannot be looked up by name or email, because neither is ever collected. What identifies an assessment is the link you are given at the end.

Being straight about what this is not. Collecting little is not the same as being secure, and this is a prototype rather than a hardened product. Treat your results link as semi-public: anyone who has it can open your results. Do not put anything in this assessment you would mind a stranger reading.

Feature six

The categories come from an argument, not a hunch

Most personality tests pick their dimensions by convention or by factor analysis of older tests. The six areas here were chosen for a specific reason: they are where honesty is most expensive, and expense is what makes a signal believable.

That reasoning has a traceable lineage. It starts with fieldwork on birds, becomes a general principle about signalling, and gets applied to human self-knowledge in a book published by Oxford University Press.

Where this differs from an ordinary quiz

None of the differences below are about being cleverer. They follow from one decision: treating your answers as evidence about a person with an interest in the outcome.

A typical personality quiz This assessment
What it does with your answers Takes them at face value and averages them. Estimates how much they were shaded, then adjusts each area by that estimate.
What the questions ask Whether you have a trait. What you do in the situations where having the trait is expensive.
What you are shown A label, and a paragraph about the label. Both score sets, the size of the correction, the label, and how well it fits.
Where the categories come from Convention, or the structure of older tests. A published argument about why people flatter themselves, with a traceable lineage.
What it says about itself That it is accurate. That it is a prototype, and exactly where it stops being trustworthy.

The honest caveat, kept in view. This is a research prototype, not a validated psychological test and not a clinical instrument. It should not inform decisions about hiring, admissions, care, or anyone's standing. The full limits are here.
